THE EVERYDAY FRICTION

The problem

One home. Too many things to remember.

Managing a home means juggling utility bills, meter readings, property taxes, insurance renewals, technical inspections, maintenance, and important documents.

Bills in your inbox Documents in a drawer Dates in your head Maintenance on a sticky note
The problem isn’t a lack of apps. It’s that managing a single home requires too many disconnected tools and too much manual effort.

01 / THE STARTING POINT

A home creates obligations. The records live everywhere.

Our starting hypothesis was that people manage a home across provider apps, messages, paper documents, and memory. Bills, repairs, move-in condition, and rental responsibilities can become hard to reconstruct when a deadline or disagreement arrives. This was the problem framing behind FirstFlat, not a finding established before discovery.

Problem hypothesis

People need a dependable way to know what is due, find proof of what happened, and understand which expenses require a conversation. We looked for recent behavior and concrete consequences rather than asking whether an app sounded useful.

Initial solution hypothesis

A home dashboard combining documents, payments, maintenance, and reminders, with optional AI extraction of dates and obligations for user review. The questionnaire tests the underlying problems; it does not test whether this proposed solution is the right answer.

Discovery sequence: investigate existing behavior → identify the most painful job → test a narrow workflow → test commitment to a paid pilot. The survey provides the first layer. Prototype observations and selling remain next steps.

02 / CUSTOMER DISCOVERY

Three perspectives on the same home.

We segmented by responsibility, not just demographics: tenants dealing with contracts and deposits; owners living in their own home managing recurring bills; and individual landlords coordinating handovers, repairs, and rent. These groups can share records but have different jobs and incentives. The supplied responses do not establish demand from professional managers, developers, insurers, or other B2B partners.

  1. Recruit in relevant communities.

    The team reports distributing the form in Facebook groups for owners and tenants and relevant subreddits such as r/imobiliare. These are contextually relevant channels for rental and housing problems. Post links and screenshots were not supplied, so channel reach, response rate, and which channel produced each answer remain unknown.

  2. Collect behavior before pitching features.

    The uploaded export records responses between 30 October and 1 November 2026. Questions cover the last rental, incidents in the past year, current record keeping, costs, and workarounds. Role-specific columns allow separate analysis. The original form URL and routing configuration are not available; the export is the evidence for the questions and answers.

  3. Clean before drawing conclusions.

    We use the primary role to assign branches. A tenant row also contains owner-branch answers; those extra answers are excluded from owner findings. Empty cells are missing, not “no.” Some follow-up answers do not match their preceding yes/no question; we do not use them to inflate incident rates. Photo-location answers are not treated as proof of losing photos because that question also has answers from people who did not report taking photos.

  4. Separate the evidence layers.

    The XLSX is the quantitative source; the PDF renders the same responses and is not a second sample. Interview audio is available below, but no verified transcript or interview notes were supplied. We do not turn the recording into invented quotes, themes, or additional survey responses.

Scope: a small, self-selected exploratory sample. Recall, selection bias, uneven segments, and incomplete follow-ups limit what we can conclude. The results describe these responses, not the Romanian housing market. No controlled comparison or causal relationship was measured.

Financial friction is concrete

About 40% of tenant answers report a partial or full deposit deduction. Among those who stated an amount, reported deductions range from 200 to 2,000 lei; repair follow-ups mention 200–300 lei. These are sparse recollections, not average losses or proof that deductions were unjustified.

Half of tenant answers describe a damage-related discussion or dispute; some were resolved easily and some were tense. A product can help retain evidence without promising to eliminate disagreement.

There is counterevidence, too

About 25% of tenant answers select none of the listed bill incidents. Not everyone has the same urgency. Owner-occupiers already use provider apps, and some report relatively little time spent on bills. Switching costs and an adequate existing solution may outweigh our value proposition.

We should recruit people with a recent, specific difficulty rather than assume every household needs another app.

Resident owners: consolidate carefully

Provider apps are the most common payment-tracking method in this branch, with email commonly used for receipts. Missed deadlines and duplicate payments appear, but so does “none of the above.” Most time answers fall in the 15–60 minute monthly band; a longer band also appears. This suggests testing retrieval and coordination, not claiming hours of guaranteed savings.

Landlords: a shared record may help

Individual landlords describe handover reports, phone or WhatsApp communication, and tracking via a notebook, phone notes, Excel, or no tracking. Damage disputes range from easy to difficult. An open answer describes cleaning costs and another concern is arrears and tenant reliability. These suggest a record-keeping workflow; software cannot guarantee a reliable tenant or recover unpaid rent.

Unproven assumptions: the form did not present FirstFlat, compare prototypes, test AI preference, ask a FirstFlat price question, or collect purchase commitments. Existing-tool payment answers are sparse and do not establish willingness to pay for this product. We have no verified acquisition, retention, conversion, or revenue results.

04 / QUALITATIVE MATERIAL

Keep the original conversation accessible.

The existing asset is included as interview material. Its filename is not proof of the interview date or participant segment. A transcript, participant context, actual interview guide, and time-coded findings are still needed before using it to substantiate specific claims. The survey findings above stand independently of this recording.

Proposed follow-up interview guide · not questions claimed to have been asked

Start with a recent incident; show the concept only after understanding the current workflow. Ask permission before recording and record the participant’s role without publishing identifying details.

  1. Tell me about the last time you were unsure who should pay for something in your home. What happened next?
  2. How did you find the agreement, invoice, messages, or proof of payment? Where did you look first?
  3. What did the incident cost in money, time, or stress? What can you verify from your records?
  4. How do you currently track payments and shared costs? Can you walk through a recent example without sharing sensitive data?
  5. What already works well? Which tools have you stopped using, and why?
  6. What happened the last time you needed move-in photos or a handover report?
  7. After the problem interview: try finding a payment proof and creating a confirmed reminder in the prototype. Where do you hesitate?
  8. What would stop you from inviting the other person or storing a document here? What would make a paid pilot worth considering?

05 / INSIGHTS → PRODUCT DIRECTION

A smaller first product. A stronger reason to use it.

These are prioritization decisions informed by the supplied responses, not features already built or tested. The current platform remains an illustrative demo.

From evidence to a testable MVP
Observed signalProduct decision & next test
Unclear expense responsibility; contracts rarely reopenedPrioritize a searchable home agreement and expense record with links to the source clause or document. Test whether a user can locate relevant evidence; do not offer an automated legal verdict.
Memory-based tracking; payment-proof difficultiesStart with a shared payment ledger, receipt attachment, and user-confirmed reminders. Test retrieval of a previous payment and completion of a real recurring obligation.
Move-in documentation and deposit disputesTest a dated handover checklist with photos, acknowledgements, and an exportable history. Keep original evidence accessible; do not claim legal protection or guaranteed deposit recovery.
Existing provider apps and low-friction usersReduce manual setup and test whether importing or linking records earns a place alongside current tools. Begin with people who have a recent painful incident, not a blanket household audience.
No direct evidence for AI demandDefer a broad AI assistant, integrations, and marketplace. First compare manual entry with suggested extraction, always requiring confirmation. Measure task success before adding automation.

First segment to investigate further: tenants and individual landlords with a recent disputed expense, handover, or missing payment record. This is a recruitment hypothesis suggested by the answers, not a statistically proven best customer segment.

06 / ARE WE READY TO TEST SELLING?

Ready for a focused experiment.
Not yet for a demand claim.

We have enough directional problem evidence to justify a narrow prototype and invite discovery participants to a pilot. We do not yet have enough evidence to say that FirstFlat is validated, that users prefer it to their current tools, or that the proposed subscriptions will sell.

  1. Close the evidence gaps.

    Retain recruitment post links, verify the original form routing, and document the audio with a time-coded transcript. Follow up on recent incidents and deliberately include people whose current approach works well.

  2. Observe the solution in use.

    Test a receipt, shared expense, and reminder workflow with tenants and landlords separately. Record completion without prompting, time to retrieve proof, onboarding effort, and objections. Do not count a compliment as task success.

  3. Run a transparent paid-pilot offer.

    Once the core workflow is usable, show a clear offer and actual price to eligible participants. Distinguish viewing the offer, joining a waitlist, booking onboarding, accepting a pilot, and paying. The existing demo is not a purchase test.

PROPOSED NEXT-ROUND GATES

Set targets before the next test: at least 80% completion of the core task without help; at least 50% of activated pilot households returning to record or retrieve a real item within 30 days; and at least 20% of qualified offer recipients accepting a paid pilot at the stated price. These are proposed decision rules, not observed results or criteria retrospectively applied to this survey. Report denominators and reasons for refusal when evaluating that pilot.

Decision: continue Customer Discovery while testing the smallest useful solution. Move into a limited selling experiment when the workflow works and an offer can be delivered honestly; expand only after observing actual commitment and repeat use.
